Orange liqueur is a sweetened spirit flavored with orange peel.
It’s essential for classics like the [margarita](/drinks/margarita) and [sidecar](/drinks/sidecar), and it shows up in a surprising number of other drinks as well.

There is a surprising variety of styles of orange liqueur.
They aren’t all the same, but they’re close enough that a single “orange liqueur” bottle will get you through most recipes.

In my recipes, I've tried to strike a balance between keeping things simple and providing
guidance on the preferred style for each recipe.
I distinguish between two categories of orange liqueur:

* **[Triple sec](/tags/triple-sec)** for bright, clean orange flavor — typical of daisies and citrus-forward sours
* **[Curaçao](/tags/curacao)** for a richer, rounder orange — better with brandy, rum, and other aged spirits

You can almost always substitute one for the other and still have a delicious drink.
But you will be able to further refine your drinks if have both on hand, as well
as a dry curaçao (more on that below).

## Triple Sec

Triple sec is the brighter, cleaner style, and is generally clear in color.
It’s usually made with a neutral spirit base, so what you taste is mostly orange — not brandy, spice, or vanilla underneath.

Cointreau is the gold standard here, though it's rather pricey.
Combier is another excellent option, but a
$15 grocery-store triple sec is fine most of the time.

I prefer triple sec in drinks like a [Margarita](/drinks/margarita), [Corpse Reviver No. 2](/drinks/corpse-reviver-2), or [White Lady](/drinks/white-lady), where you want the orange to be bright and citrusy.

## Curaçao

Curaçao is the older style, historically made with peels of the bitter Laraha orange from the island of Curaçao.
Today the name covers a broader set of orange liqueurs that tend to be rounder and a bit more complex than a typical triple sec — sometimes with subtle spice or herbal notes alongside the orange.

When I want a curaçao, I generally reach for a brandy-based bottles like Grand Marnier and Gran Gala.
These bring a cognac warmth, vanilla, and toffee that a triple sec simply doesn’t have.
That richness is great in some drinks and a little heavy-handed in others.

Curaçao is usually my preference in drinks that contain aged spirits — [El Presidente](/drinks/el-presidente), [Mai Tai](/drinks/mai-tai), or [Egg Sour](/drinks/egg-sour).

### Dry Curaçao

Some recipes call specifically for a *dry* curaçao.
Pierre Ferrand Dry Curaçao is really the only option here.
It’s less candy-sweet than a basic orange liqueur, with more complex, bitter orange peel flavor.

Dry curaçao can really make a drink like a [Sidecar](/drinks/sidecar) stand out,
but feel free to substitute a different orange liqueur if that's what you have on hand.
When you do, consider slightly reducing the amount of syrup or other sweet ingredients
in the drink.

## What to buy

If you’re only getting one bottle, start with a decent triple sec.
It’s the most versatile, and it’s what most recipes historically meant by “orange liqueur” anyway.
Cointreau if you want to spend a bit more; something cheaper if you don’t.

When you’re ready for a second bottle, pick up a dry curaçao (Pierre Ferrand is excellent) or a brandy-based option like Grand Marnier or Gran Gala, depending on which drinks you make most.
